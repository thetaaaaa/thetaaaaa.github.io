南京大学图书情报学博士生，现在在哈佛量化社会科学研究所（IQSS）做 fellow。

这里写两类东西。一类是在美国的生活观察——看到的、不太习惯的、想了一阵才明白过来的事。另一类是方法：一个数据集从零到能用要经过什么，配套的程序怎么写，哪些规范值得守，哪些工具真的省时间。这类文章多半拿 CBDB（中国历代人物传记资料库）当例子，因为它是我手上最熟的一个，也因为它的坑足够多，讲清楚一个就能省别人半天。

为什么写方法这一类，说明一下。我对流程的规范性大概有点执拗，总想把每个环节的最佳实践找出来再动手。硕士期间我长期做计算人文方向的研究：一边开发和评测基于机器学习、神经网络模型的古文自动化处理工具，一边做中国古代文献的知识组织与知识挖掘。那几年最深的教训是：开头不规范，后面的时间就全花在修补和纠正前期工作上，而且常常补不干净，只能推倒重做。所以这里写的方法不图好看，图的是让人少走一遍我走过的弯路。也有一点私心——作为合作者，大家手上的方法都立得住，省下来的是各自的时间，也间接是我的。

中文和英文都写。这两套对话之间的空隙，是我觉得最有意思的地方。

I'm a PhD student in library and information science at Nanjing University, currently a fellow at Harvard's Institute for Quantitative Social Science (IQSS).

Two kinds of writing live here. One is observation — a year of living in the United States, and the things that took me a while to understand. The other is method: what it actually takes to get a dataset from nothing to usable, how to write the code around it, which conventions are worth following, and which tools genuinely save time. Most of those notes use CBDB (the China Biographical Database) as the worked example, because it's the project I know best and because it has enough pitfalls that explaining one saves someone else an afternoon.

A word on why the method half exists. I care, probably more than is reasonable, about the discipline of a workflow — I want to find the best practice for each step before starting. My master's work was in computational humanities — what is more often called digital humanities: developing and evaluating machine-learning and neural-network tools for the automated processing of classical Chinese, and working on knowledge organization and knowledge mining over premodern Chinese sources. The lesson that stuck is this: when the beginning isn't disciplined, all the time afterwards goes into patching and correcting the early work — and often it can't be patched, only redone. So these notes aren't written to look tidy; they're written so someone else doesn't have to take the detour I took. There is some self-interest in it too — as a collaborator, when the conventions everyone works by hold up, the time saved is their own, and indirectly mine.

I write in both Chinese and English; the gap between those two conversations is the part I find most interesting.
